# Assess quality gaps: what to bring to the investigation

## A good investigation

A good investigation covers every row in scope, as the measurement plan sets it, because a Low or
unrated row can still hold what stops the release.

What the agent says it will do, before starting:

- what it can do from where it is: read what exists, run what is already installed in a scratch
  copy, try disposable cases;
- what needs the user's yes first: anything fetched or installed, anything reaching a live service
  or someone's account;
- what it cannot do: what waits on someone else, on the release being in people's hands, or on a
  person's judgment.

Something that changes the picture goes to the user before the agent builds on it: a fault nobody
expected, an observation that contradicts an assumed fact or a bar, a priority no row covers.
Comparisons that depend on it stay conditional until it is settled.

The user's turns go on what only they can give: what they have seen, a judgment the findings call
for, a yes to a check that reaches past the project. Someone with an evening reads the findings
once, answers once, and knows where their release stands.

## Ideas to bring

- **Look at what exists before calling a row unknown.** Read what the project's documents, code and
  tests say about it; asking the user where the records are does not replace looking. A pre-read
  finding that bears on a row is a reading for it. Say what was read with each finding.
- **What the pre-read saw running is an observation for its row,** with its evidence folder linked
  from the finding. Don't run it again unless something has changed since, such as the code or the
  conditions, because the user has already seen it and a second run adds nothing.
- **What a finding rests on decides what it can claim.** A test report, something observed, a
  deduction and a proposed experiment support different claims.
  - Reading the code or documents is investigation, welcome when nothing can run; say that it was
    reading. It can show Below bar where what the bar asks for has not been built, even before a
    planned check that still waits has run; the finding then says it was reading and what that
    check would add. It can meet a
    bar about what a document or message contains, without showing that anyone read it. It cannot
    show At bar for how the product behaves under real conditions, or how it feels to use.
  - What the agent sees running in a scratch copy is an observation under those conditions; give
    the version and the conditions.
  - None of these is a person having used it.
- **Confidence belongs to the exact claim and its evidence.** That only an agent checked something,
  or that the user agrees, doesn't by itself raise or cap it; say when only an agent checked it.
  Polished code and a sure author are not evidence.
- **Testing is finding out what is actually true.** "The tests pass" does not mean it was tested:
  the project's own tests show what they check and no more, and do not outweigh a failure found
  another way.
  - When something odd turns up, chase it within the question the user agreed, then make a check
    for what was found. Once a fault is found, look near it, because faults cluster.
  - Distrust a check that can't fail for the reason that matters. Changing the expected result
    because the output changed is not investigating.
- **Who investigates.** Not the agent that built the thing, because it shares the frame that made
  the fault. Some testing needs a person; say which part. The investigation can go to a separate
  agent, as [when one skill opens
  another](../../../references/opening-another-skill.md) says for a
  subagent:
  - give it the product itself, the users and their situations, the questions with their bars and
    ways of telling, and what needs a yes;
  - have it report what was tried and found, the evidence, and what is still unknown;
  - check that against the records before the user sees it, and say whose observation it was;
  - read its working as well as its report, for hedges and things it let go; what it saw once
    stays seen once.
- **What the builder says against what is seen.** Agreement can support a narrow conclusion;
  disagreement is something to look into.
- **When the uncertainty affects a decision,** say what could overturn the conclusion and which
  observation would most raise confidence, often a cheap one. Finding out can show the gap smaller
  or larger than feared, not only change how sure anyone is.
- **What a gap or an unknown puts at risk.** Urgency depends on timing, on what depends on what, and
  on the cost of finding out or acting, as well as on how much a priority matters and how far it
  falls short.
  - Low confidence with high stakes can deserve more attention than a known gap, because the
    unknown may hide a bigger one.
  - Doubt about the bar can make a well-supported gap the wrong target.
  - For a miss, keep how often, how much and what can be tolerated. A small miss, or one on a Low
    row, can still defeat what the release is for.
  - If an apparent failure protects another bar, explain the trade-off.
  - Changing a bar is the user's choice, not a way to make the assessment look better.
  - The pattern, where risk gathers and what one piece of finding out would settle together, might
    be several unknowns in one workflow, one conversation that could clarify several bars, or an
    installation that fails and blocks other observations.
