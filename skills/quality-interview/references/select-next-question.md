# Choosing the next question

**Goal.** Choose a question that helps the user think through what matters to them.

**Done when.** One question is chosen that fits the conversation and makes clear what the agent
wants to understand. It lets the user answer in their own words and decide whether to go on.

Work from the conversation and the topics recorded so far (`status` lists them with their next
questions), especially what the user said last. The [opening questions](opening-questions.md) and
the [follow-up questions](follow-up-questions.md) are examples, not a script: reword them in the
user's language, or ask a different question when it would serve them better.

Ask open questions that the user can answer in their own words, such as "What would happen then?"
Don't offer answers to choose from, or wording to agree with, because what they choose goes on
record as their words.

What you take from this guide back to the interview is one question, under the number `next`
gives, or under its own earlier number when you go back to an open question; or else [a quick look
at named files](#5-a-quick-look-at-named-files). Deciding whether to stop, and saving, are done by
the interview's own steps, not here.

A question the agent would like to come back to can go in `notes.md` in the sitting folder,
because that survives a pause. It is asked when the conversation gives room for it, not because it
was noted ([coming back to a question](#3-coming-back-to-a-question)).

## 1. The first round

**Goal.** Give the user's concerns, and the people who matter, room before the conversation
narrows.

**Done when.** The user has described their concerns, and the people who matter, well enough that
more broad questions would mostly repeat what has been heard. Concerns they haven't raised, or have
put off, can stay open; nobody has pushed them for an answer.

Work only from the way the user describes things. Treat what the project's files suggest as a
guess: the user is the authority on who matters, what is valuable and important, the parts of the
product and the goals. Save questions about quality dimensions, how to classify a concern, its
causes and its fixes for after the first round.

When changing subject, first respond briefly to something specific in their last answer. Then
give room to whichever point of view still needs it, with an opening question, or a follow-up such
as [repeating their own words](follow-up-questions.md#repeat-their-own-words) that doesn't narrow
the round. If a broad question about who matters stalls, offer the three circles from the opening
questions.

Some questions are worth considering while the conversation is still broad, because they are harder
to ask once it has narrowed:

- **Room for a group of people** who might matter and hasn't had any yet. Don't ask the user to rule
  a group in or out when nothing suggests it is involved.
- **A concern they haven't raised.** Inviting anything else lets them say what the questions so
  far haven't reached.
- **The bad case**: what going badly would look like from here, if they haven't described it
  already. It is worth offering early, because people who won't list their hard limits will often
  describe an outcome they refuse to accept, and asked only at the end it comes after the
  conversation has narrowed. A story about something that already went wrong, such as why someone
  stopped using the product, is a good lead-in to the question but doesn't answer it.
- **The good case** for the project as a whole: what going well would look like. It also fits among
  the later questions about what good would look like, so it can wait.

Weigh these against what the user has shown they want to talk about and how much room they have.
None is asked because this list names it. Someone who came with one concern and has said what they
wanted to say has had a good first round without any of them.

When their bad case names something bad and it isn't clear whether they could live with it, and that
matters, the next question can be
[what would make them say "ugh"](follow-up-questions.md#what-would-make-them-say-ugh).

The first round usually takes about five questions, counting the first, but it ends when enough has
been heard, not at a count. Then open [check-ins](check-ins.md) now and offer the check-in it
describes for the end of the first round, because that one lists the topics in the user's words
rather than asking only whether to stop.

## 2. Going deeper

**Goal.** Understand a concern, why it matters and how it connects to the rest of what the user has
said.

**Done when.** The concern is understood well enough for this sitting, or the user wants to move
on. Useful follow-up questions are kept, so the topic can be left without chasing detail for its
own sake.

After the first round, first settle which topic the question is about. Stay with the current topic
until its answers stop adding anything or the user's interest moves elsewhere.

Then choose the next open topic by the interest the user has shown: they called it important, came
back to it, asked to keep exploring it, or kept building on it. Use how much it would help the
later strategy work only to break a tie. Talking at length or sounding pleased doesn't by itself
show that something matters to them. Signs of tiredness bear only on whether to stop, never on
which topic comes next.

Then choose a [follow-up question](follow-up-questions.md) for whatever is still unclear about that
topic: the facts they offer, why each matters to them, how the things they have said fit together,
or what they believe leads to what. Aim at the smallest missing piece that would help them think
further.

- When a question repeats or builds on their words, keep their hedge with it ("the key thing, you
  guessed", "kind of clunky"). A hedge dropped from a question stays dropped in every record after
  it.
- If the user says they have just realised something about the project, ask whether they want to
  look at it before changing topic.
- What they have tried, what happened, and what makes their situation difficult are worth exploring
  when they help explain the concern. They are not a new stage, or a cue to suggest a fix.
- Record what the user said as their account, without asking them to say it again. If they don't
  want to explore a link the agent drew, keep it as a question; don't finish their meaning for them.
- Use the [questions about quality](follow-up-questions.md#questions-about-quality) (what good would
  look like, how they would know, how it is now) only when the user's interest makes them
  relevant, and in their language. Don't steer the conversation towards them. What to do about it
  is for the user, or agents they name, to say. If the user talks about a trade-off, ask what they
  would refuse to give up.

The end of a topic, a move to a different one, and the start of the later questions about what good
would look like are natural pauses, where a check-in can fit.

**When the conversation has run out of steam.** This check-in may be offered when both of these
are true:

- after a new angle has been tried, the next two answers add nothing new: no new point,
  correction, sign of interest or invitation to follow up;
- no open topic still has a question their interest supports.

Refusals, and answers that put something off-limits, don't count towards this.

To offer it, open [check-ins](check-ins.md) now and offer the one it describes for running out of
steam.

## 3. Coming back to a question

**Goal.** Give the user another chance at something that seemed to matter and was left open, when
the conversation has room for it.

**Done when.** The question has been asked fairly and their answer respected, or the conversation
went where the user wanted without it.

Two kinds of question are worth coming back to when they fit what the user is talking about, such
as at the end of a topic: one asked and left open, and one the agent noted it would like to ask.
Either is the agent's wish, not something the user owes, so a check-in or a close doesn't wait for
it. An answer, an honest refusal or "nothing" settles either. A question they turned aside is
declined: keep the gap rather than asking it again in other words, as
[each turn](../../../references/each-turn.md) says.

If they stop first, the summary keeps the two kinds apart, because a later reader needs to know what
the user was asked. A question put to them and still unanswered stays a question asked and left
unanswered. One never put to them is the agent's open question, not one they left unanswered.

**A second look at dealbreakers** is one such question, with the
[dealbreaker questions](follow-up-questions.md#what-would-be-a-dealbreaker). A guarded person often
gives a safe non-answer early, when they have been heard least, and names a real limit once they
feel heard. It tends to help when:

- they hinted at a limit, or named one with a hedge ("the real dealbreakers I think are…");
- a trade-off under discussion makes it relevant;
- their early answer about what going badly would look like was thin, and they have since said more.

Don't repeat the general question about failure. Ask about what they named, openly, and let their
answer show how bad it would be: "You said losing his progress might be a real dealbreaker. What
would happen if he lost it?" If they have already said what would happen, ask about their own words
for it: "What would 'the end of us' look like?"

If what they describe sounds like something they would dislike but live with, or it is hard to
tell, follow with [what would make them say
"ugh"](follow-up-questions.md#what-would-make-them-say-ugh). Keep their words, and their hedge if
they keep it, and don't call it a dealbreaker unless they do.

## 4. Going back to earlier answers

**Goal.** Let the user change or add to something they said earlier.

**Done when.** The change is understood well enough to carry forward, and the earlier
understanding is treated as still open to change.

A correction that fits the current exchange goes in that reply's topics. When going back takes the
conversation away from its current topic, note in `notes.md` the question the conversation was
heading towards, so it can be returned to. A new question about an earlier topic gets a new number,
like any other. Let the user's interest decide whether to ask more about the earlier topic or
return to the noted question.

## 5. A quick look at named files

After the first round, when an open topic would benefit from project facts the user would otherwise
have to explain, follow [a quick look at named files](quick-look.md). Hand back its permission
question as this turn's one unnumbered question or, when they have already given permission, the
look itself. Then choose the next question from what the look found or, if they declined, from what
they said.
