# Quality interview: opening questions

Questions for the rest of the first round, chosen as [choosing the next
question](select-next-question.md) describes. None comes first: choose the one that belongs in the
conversation.

As you choose, keep track of who has had room:

- the user's own experience;
- the people who build the project;
- agents that use or work on it;
- the people who use or rely on it;
- funders or sponsors, where relevant;
- anyone else affected.

Give the builders' experience the same standing as the users', and let the conversation show which
groups need attention. Consider roles agents might play where something points to them or the user
says they might. Don't assume agents are involved, or ask the user to explain why they aren't.

## Questions for the first round

### Understand what is happening

**Goal.** Invite the user's view of the present situation before deciding whether it is a problem.
Use this when the first question brought a broad judgment but little sense of what is happening.

- "What's going on here that I should understand?"
- "What's actually happening right now?"
- "What has surprised you about this project or how people experience it?"

### Make room for day-to-day friction

**Goal.** Understand how the project affects the day-to-day experience of the user and the people
doing the work. Use this when other people's needs have dominated the conversation, or when a
general problem has been named but its effect on people is still unclear.

- "What about this project is causing you the most pain or friction right now?"
- "What is hardest about building, supporting, or changing this project for the people doing that
  work?"
- "How does that affect you?"

### Widen the circle of people who matter

**Goal.** Make room for people and agents that use, build, fund, support, depend on, or otherwise
deal with the project. Use this when familiar groups have dominated, or another affected person
needs room.

- "Who matters here?"
- "What stands out to you about the experience of people who use or rely on this project?"
- "What matters most to the people funding, sponsoring, or otherwise depending on this project?"
- "Whose life or work is affected by this project in ways we have not talked about yet?"

If a broad question feels too big, as when the user laughs, stalls or asks how ambitious to be,
offer three circles in the project's own terms:

- the people closest to the work;
- the next people out, who come across it;
- the most distant people it could one day affect.

Ask where they would like to start. Name only the circles, so the user names everyone in them.

### Leave room for concerns not yet raised

**Goal.** Give the user a chance to bring up concerns the conversation has not reached yet. Use this
when one or two topics have dominated, or just before the first round ends. They may have nothing
to add.

- "What other concerns are you carrying about the project?"
- "What else about this project is on your mind?"
- "What would you hate for us to overlook about this project?"

### Picture what good would look like

**Goal.** Understand what success would look like to the user, in their own words, before you bring
in any ideas of your own about quality. Use this once they have begun to describe their concerns
and have not yet said what going well would mean.

- "If this project went really well, what would you see happening?"
- "What would tell you this had turned out the way you wanted?"
- "A year from now, what would make you glad about how this went?"

### Picture what bad would look like

**Goal.** Hear what failure looks like to the user. Limits and concerns people don't volunteer tend
to come out here. It matters as much as the good case and is worth offering early, before the
conversation narrows ([choosing the next question](select-next-question.md#1-the-first-round) says
why).

- "And from here, if it went badly, what would that look like?"
- "What would make you regret how this went?"
- "What would you consider unacceptable, however well everything else turned out?"

### Notice what is already valuable

**Goal.** Hear what works, what people care about, and what any change should keep. Use this when
problems have dominated, or when what they are proud of and value needs room.

- "What do you think is great about this project as it stands today?"
- "What is working well that you would not want to lose?"
- "What matters most to protect as this project grows or changes?"

### Look ahead

**Goal.** Let the user describe the difference they hope the project will make, before any talk of
solutions or plans. Use this once their present concerns are clear and the conversation is ready
for what they hope for, or within a well-understood topic when they are ready to look ahead.

- "What would you like to have happen?"
- "What does success look like for you?"
- "What do you hope this project will make possible for the people it touches?"
